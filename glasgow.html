11:55

Before you start

You're standing outside Motel One, beside Central Station. Before you set off, a quick word about the city you're about to walk.

Glasgow got rich on trade. First tobacco in the seventeen hundreds, then shipbuilding and heavy industry on the Clyde. The money had to go somewhere. It went into big, confident Victorian buildings, and the odd very good joke on top of a statue.

Today's a loop. We'll climb to a cathedral, wander a hill full of Victorian dead, and be back here before dark. Wear shoes with grip, because the paths up there are steep.

Right. Head up Buchanan Street towards the Gallery of Modern Art. It's about ten minutes.

12:00

1Gallery of Modern Art and the Duke of Wellington

What to look for

  • The traffic cone on the Duke's head, and whether it's gold, glitter or plain orange today
  • The horse and rider on the pavement in front of the building
  • The grand old townhouse frontage behind the statue

Stop in Royal Exchange Square, on the Queen Street side, in front of the Gallery of Modern Art. Find the horseman on the pavement. That's the Duke of Wellington, and he's usually wearing a traffic cone. Look up.

The cone is a long running tradition. The council keeps removing it, and it keeps coming back. The BBC reported a council plea about it in two thousand and five. Glasgow listened politely and carried on.

The cone has had its moments. It's been painted gold for the twenty twelve Olympics, and again for the twenty fourteen Commonwealth Games. Once, a hotel chain swapped it for a glitter cone as a promotion.

The statue itself is serious. Carlo Marochetti made it, and it went up in eighteen forty four. Now look at the building behind. It began as the townhouse of a tobacco lord, William Cunninghame of Lainshaw, in the late seventeen seventies.

Later it became the Royal Exchange, then a library. In nineteen ninety six it opened as a gallery. It's free, open today until five, and billed as the most visited modern art gallery in Scotland. Pop in if you like, but watch the clock. Then walk on to George Square, about five minutes away.

12:20

2George Square and the City Chambers

What to look for

  • The City Chambers on the east side, and how much stone it takes to look this pleased with itself
  • The cenotaph for the two world wars
  • The statues dotted around the square

Stand in the middle of George Square and turn to face the long building on the east side. That's the City Chambers, home of Glasgow City Council. The architect, William Young, finished it in eighteen eighty eight.

It was built to show off. Inside are marble staircases and a banqueting hall, and the style borrows from classical Italian architecture. Glasgow had the money and wanted visitors to know it. Subtle wasn't the plan. It was a statement in stone, and the whole city was meant to read it.

There are free tours on weekdays, at half past ten and half past two. The afternoon one clashes with the cathedral, so today you're getting the outside only. Think of it as the trailer.

Then look around the square. There are statues everywhere, and a cenotaph for the Glaswegians who served in the two world wars. The mood shifts there, and the pigeons seem to know it. Money built the Chambers, but the cenotaph is about what people gave.

Queen Street station is only a few minutes' walk from here, which is handy to know. Now it's lunchtime. Head for the Merchant City, about ten minutes along Ingram Street. It's where the tobacco money once lived.

12:45

3Lunch in the Merchant City

What to look for

  • The old stonework above the modern shopfronts, which is where the grand floors hide
  • How many different floors and window styles you can spot on one street

You've reached the Merchant City. Find a table, take the weight off, and order something warm. While the food's on its way, think about who built all this.

In the seventeen hundreds, merchants made fortunes importing tobacco. They were called the tobacco lords, and they built this district to match their bank balances. Big houses, big warehouses, big opinions. They wanted the street to say, loudly, that business was good.

Then the American War of Independence arrived, and the trade collapsed. The party was over. It's a very Glasgow lesson, and a fair warning to anyone spending big. Enjoy the good times, and keep a coat handy.

Today the district is cafes, restaurants and bars. The merchants have gone, and the lunch crowd has the buildings. The lords would approve of the food, if not the prices.

Take your time. The cathedral has been waiting since the twelfth century, and it can manage another half hour. After you've eaten, walk east to Glasgow Cross, then up the High Street. It's about twenty minutes, so aim to reach the cathedral by quarter to two. Keep climbing. At the top sits a building that's been there far longer than any tobacco lord.

13:45

4Glasgow Cathedral

What to look for

  • The twelfth century stonework on the outside, and how weathered it looks
  • St Mungo's tomb in the lower church
  • The organ, if it's playing or if it's quiet

Stop at the front of Glasgow Cathedral and look up at the stone. It dates from the twelfth century. Some call it the High Kirk, or St Mungo's. It's described as the oldest cathedral on the Scottish mainland, and the oldest building in Glasgow.

Here's the surprise. At the Reformation, altars, sculptures and Catholic furnishings were stripped out of churches. This one kept its medieval building. One source calls its survival a miracle. It's also described as the most complete medieval cathedral on the mainland.

The ground matters too. St Mungo, also known as Kentigern, is Glasgow's patron saint. He's thought to have been buried here in the year six hundred and twelve. His tomb is in the lower church, so go down and find it.

Practical bits. Entry is free, and donations go to Historic Environment Scotland. Winter hours run to four, with last entry about three, and the lower church closes about quarter past three. The audio guide costs two pounds.

The Willis organ dates from eighteen seventy nine, and was rebuilt in nineteen ninety six. It can be loud, so don't jump. When you're done, find the footbridge beside the cathedral. It leads up to the Necropolis.

14:30

5Glasgow Necropolis

What to look for

  • The Knox statue on top of its column
  • The grandest monuments near the summit compared with the plainer graves lower down
  • The cathedral spread out below the hill

Cross the footbridge and climb until you're standing beside the tall column with John Knox on top. It's fifty eight feet high. It went up in eighteen twenty five, before the cemetery even existed. Knox isn't buried here.

The cemetery came next. The Merchants' House of Glasgow planned it from eighteen thirty one, with Dr John Strang as its advocate. It opened in eighteen thirty three, modelled on Père Lachaise in Paris. It's Scotland's first garden cemetery.

Notice the pecking order. The richest families paid for plots near the summit, by Knox. Humbler graves sit on the lower terraces. Pay for the good spot, and you get the good spot. Even in death, Glasgow's merchants wanted the view.

There are roughly fifty thousand burials here, and around three and a half thousand monuments and tombs. In the north west corner, a Jewish burial ground was established in eighteen thirty two.

It's free to walk. Take a moment at the top and look back at the cathedral below. Take the paths carefully, because they're steep and can be slippery. Aim to be heading down by about half past three, before dusk, and then walk back to the hotel.

15:30

6Back to the hotel

That's the loop. It's about thirty minutes back on foot, mostly downhill. Go through the Merchant City, or via George Square and Buchanan Street, or grab a taxi if your legs vote for it.

Rest at the hotel, then eat early. Aim for the train from Central low level to the Exhibition Centre at about half past five, for Shinedown at the OVO Hydro.

Think back over today. A tobacco lord's townhouse, a council showpiece, a hill of merchants' graves, and a duke in a traffic cone. Glasgow turned its money into buildings, and then made jokes about them.

Tonight, it's your turn to spend it loudly. Enjoy the show.
