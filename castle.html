Before you go in

What to look for

  • The gatehouse straight ahead, and the long slope of the Esplanade you are standing on.
  • Behind you, the Royal Mile runs downhill along the ridge towards Holyrood.

Welcome to Edinburgh Castle. You are standing on the Esplanade, and before we go anywhere, look at the shape of the ground.

The rock in front of you is the plug of an extinct volcano. It formed about three hundred and fifty million years ago. The molten rock cooled into something very hard called dolerite, and when glaciers ground across Scotland much later, they couldn't wear it down. They flowed around it instead, and they scraped the softer rock to the east into a long tail. Geologists call it a crag and tail. The crag is the castle rock. The tail is the ridge you just walked up, and the Royal Mile is built along its spine. That one accident of geology is why Old Town Edinburgh is where it is.

People noticed the rock early. A Welsh poem from around the year six hundred, called Y Gododdin, mentions a stronghold named Din Eidyn, and most historians take that to mean this rock. In six thirty eight, Irish records say the Angles under Oswald of Northumbria besieged it. So when you walk in, you're joining a very old queue.

Here is a number to carry with you. Research in twenty fourteen counted twenty six sieges in eleven hundred years. That gives the castle a fair claim to being the most besieged place in Great Britain.

The Esplanade itself is much younger. It was laid out as a parade ground in seventeen fifty three and extended in eighteen forty five. Before that, a sixteenth century earthwork called the Spur stood on this spot, one of the earliest angled bastions of its kind in Britain.

Your ticket is for twelve thirty, so use the next few minutes to look around. The plan for the rest of the visit is a loop. Be at the One O'Clock Gun by five to one, and be walking out by about a quarter past three. Tap Next at each stop, or tap Find my stop and the page will pick the closest one.

1Gatehouse and drawbridge

What to look for

  • The two statues in the niches either side of the gate: Robert the Bruce and William Wallace.

This is the gatehouse. It looks medieval, but it's Victorian. The present gatehouse was built in eighteen eighty eight, part of a big restoration that gave the castle much of the romantic look you see today.

The two statues are Robert the Bruce on one side and William Wallace on the other, and they're here for a reason. Edward the First of England captured this castle in twelve ninety six, after a three day bombardment. Then, on the night of the fourteenth of March thirteen fourteen, a Scottish force led by Bruce's nephew Thomas Randolph, Earl of Moray, climbed the rock up its steep north face and took it back. Bruce then ordered the defences torn down so the English could never use the castle against him again.

That was the pattern for years. The castle was captured, lost, recaptured and wrecked. David the Second rebuilt it after the Treaty of Berwick in thirteen fifty seven.

Have your tickets ready, then cross the drawbridge. From here every gate is narrower and steeper than the last. That's exactly the point.

12:38

2Portcullis Gate and the Lang Siege

What to look for

  • The Argyle Tower sitting on top of the older, plainer Portcullis Gate.
  • The tower holds the Fight for the Castle exhibition if you want a quick look.

Ahead is the Portcullis Gate, the grand entrance to the upper castle. Notice that it's two buildings stacked together. The gate underneath is old. The Scots baronial tower on top, the Argyle Tower, was added in eighteen eighty six and eighty seven by the architect Hippolyte Blanc. The money came from the Edinburgh publisher William Nelson. The tower is named after the ninth Earl of Argyll, who was held here before his execution in sixteen eighty five.

The gate itself is a result of the most dramatic episode in the castle's history, the Lang Siege. After Mary, Queen of Scots was forced off the throne, her supporters, led by Sir William Kirkcaldy of Grange, held this castle for her against the regent's forces from fifteen seventy one.

In April fifteen seventy three, about a thousand English troops arrived under Sir William Drury, with twenty seven cannons from Berwick. The bombardment began on the seventeenth of May, and over twelve days the gunners fired around three thousand shots. On the twenty second the south wall of David's Tower collapsed. The next day the Constable's Tower, the round tower that stood right where you're standing, fell and blocked the entrance. The garrison surrendered on the twenty eighth. Grange and others were hanged at the Mercat Cross on the third of August.

The Regent Morton then rebuilt the defences, and the Portcullis Gate replaced the fallen tower. That's why very few buildings in the castle are older than fifteen seventy three. If you see a medieval stone here, it survived a lot.

If you want a look inside the Argyle Tower, do it now. Otherwise walk through the gate and keep climbing.

12:45

3The Lang Stairs

What to look for

  • Count the steps. There are seventy.
  • The gate at the top is Foog's Gate, the entrance to the summit area.

Now you have a choice. The Lang Stairs are the most direct way up to the summit of the Castle Rock, and they once formed the original entrance to the castle. There are seventy steps. Count them.

The gentler cobbled road that winds around the hill was made in the sixteen hundreds, for moving heavy guns. Either way, you're heading for the top of the rock, and the top is called the Upper Ward, or the Citadel.

You enter it through Foog's Gate, a late seventeenth century gateway. Nobody knows for sure where the name comes from. It used to be called the Foggy Gate, and one idea is that it relates to the sea fogs called haars that roll in off the Forth.

Beside the gate you'll see large water cisterns. They were built so the castle depended less on its wells. A fortress that can't get water is a fortress that loses a siege, and this one had plenty of practice at sieges.

4Mons Meg

What to look for

  • The barrel is built from iron staves held by hoops, like a giant barrel.
  • Stand in front of the muzzle. The bore is about twenty inches across.

At the top you meet Mons Meg, one of the largest cannons in the world by calibre. The bore is twenty inches across, and she fired stone balls that weighed about a hundred and seventy five kilograms. The whole gun weighs roughly six to seven tonnes, depending on whose figures you trust.

She was made in Flanders by Jehan Cambier, the artillery maker to Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy. She was tested at Mons, in what's now Belgium, in June fourteen forty nine, and that's where her name comes from. Philip sent her to James the Second of Scotland as a gift in the fourteen fifties, a sign of support for the Scottish king, whose marriage he had helped arrange.

Look at how she's built. The barrel is made from twenty five long iron staves, held together by iron hoops that were shrunk on while hot. Think of a barrel made for giants.

In fourteen ninety seven, Meg was dragged down the Royal Mile to the sound of minstrels, to attack Norham Castle across the English border. After the middle of the sixteenth century she only fired on ceremonial occasions. Her barrel burst around sixteen eighty. In seventeen fifty four she was taken to the Tower of London, along with other unused weapons. Sir Walter Scott and others campaigned for her return, and she came home to Edinburgh in eighteen twenty nine.

There's an older legend that a village blacksmith made her for the siege of Threave Castle and named her after his wife, Meg, and the lands of Mollance. Historians don't take it seriously, but it's a good story.

12:53

5St Margaret's Chapel

What to look for

  • The tiny Romanesque nave and the rounded chancel arch.
  • The stained glass, installed in nineteen twenty two.

Right beside Mons Meg is St Margaret's Chapel, the oldest building in Edinburgh. It's tiny, and it has survived nearly nine hundred years of war around it.

Margaret was an English princess who fled to Scotland after the Norman conquest of ten sixty six, and around ten seventy she married King Malcolm the Third. She was famously pious. She set up a ferry across the Forth for pilgrims on their way to Dunfermline Abbey.

She died in this castle in ten ninety three, only days after hearing that her husband had been killed in battle. She was made a saint in twelve fifty.

People once assumed she prayed in this chapel, but the style of the stonework shows it was built later, under her son David the First, who reigned from eleven twenty four to eleven fifty three. He built it as a private royal chapel in her memory.

Then comes the lucky part. When Robert the Bruce's men took the castle in thirteen fourteen and had the buildings destroyed, this chapel was the only one that was spared. In thirteen twenty nine, on his deathbed, Bruce talked about Queen Margaret and ordered the chapel repaired.

After the Reformation it stopped being used for worship, and from the sixteenth century it served as a gunpowder store. By eighteen forty five it was a storeroom attached to a garrison chapel, and an antiquarian called Sir Daniel Wilson realised what it was. The garrison chapel was demolished and the little chapel was restored in eighteen fifty one and fifty two, with the support of Queen Victoria. The stained glass went in in nineteen twenty two.

Step inside for a minute or two. It's very small, and that's what makes it special.

12:58

6The One O'Clock Gun

What to look for

  • Find a spot on Mills Mount Battery with a clear view of the gun. Cover your ears if you're close.

Now to the north west corner, Mills Mount Battery, for the One O'Clock Gun. It fires at one o'clock every day except Sundays, Good Friday and Christmas Day, so today you'll hear it. Find a spot with a view of the gun and wait.

Here's why it exists. Ships anchored in the Firth of Forth and in Leith harbour needed the exact time to set their chronometers. A navigator who doesn't know the time can't work out his longitude at sea. So Edinburgh set up time signals. They were first established in the eighteen forties by Charles Piazzi Smyth, the Astronomer Royal for Scotland. A time ball was added to the Nelson Monument on Calton Hill in eighteen fifty two, and the gun has been firing from the castle since eighteen sixty one.

It's a gun and a ball because they back each other up. A sailor can see the ball drop from a long way off, in the instant it happens. Sound travels slowly, so a ship far out on the Forth would hear the bang late. Sailors learned to watch the ball. The gun was for the people who were close enough to hear it.

The gun today is a one hundred and five millimetre light gun, which came into service on the thirtieth of November two thousand and one. Before that it was a twenty five pounder howitzer. And for twenty six years, from nineteen seventy nine until his death in two thousand and five, the man who fired it was a Territorial Army gunner called Thomas McKay, known all over Edinburgh as Tam the Gun.

Look east from here at sunset, and you'll see the Nelson Monument on Calton Hill, where the ball you've just heard about still drops. That's where you'll be this afternoon.

13:05

7Argyle Battery and the view

What to look for

  • Princes Street and the New Town straight ahead.
  • The Firth of Forth beyond, and Fife on the far shore.

Right beside the gun is the Argyle Battery. It's a six gun battery from the seventeen thirties, and the cannons in place now date from about eighteen ten, the time of the Napoleonic Wars. That open outlook to the north is why it was a good place to defend the castle.

Today it's a very good place to look. You can see the New Town laid out in front of you, then Leith, the Firth of Forth, and Fife on the far shore.

Here's a story from this north wall. On the eighth of September seventeen fifteen, just two days after the first Jacobite rising began, about a hundred Highlanders led by Lord Drummond tried to climb the walls with help from some of the garrison. It nearly worked. But the rope ladder the sentries let down turned out to be too short, and then the watch changed and the alarm went up. The Jacobites ran. The soldiers inside who had helped them were hanged or flogged.

At the very base of the rock, far below, stood the Wellhouse Tower, built in thirteen sixty two to guard St Margaret's Well, a natural spring that gave the castle a second source of water. You can't see it from here, but it's a reminder that every fortress runs on water.

13:15

8The Dog Cemetery

What to look for

  • The small stones on the ledge below the summit, with names of the dogs.

Close by is a small and unexpectedly touching place, the Dogs' Cemetery. It sits on a ledge just below the summit, and it was created in the nineteenth century as a burial ground for the soldiers' regimental mascots.

Dogs have been buried here since the eighteen forties, among them Dobbler and Winkle. Some were mascots. Others were the pets of army officers, or even of the castle governor.

It's a good reminder that the castle was a working garrison. From sixteen sixty, after Charles the Second took the throne, a soldier garrison was kept here without a break, until the troops finally left for Redford Barracks in nineteen twenty three. For more than two and a half centuries, this was a place where ordinary soldiers lived and where their dogs lived with them. Take a minute.

13:30

9Crown Square and the National War Memorial

What to look for

  • The four sides of the square: the palace to the east, the Great Hall to the south, the War Memorial to the north.

Now into the heart of the castle, Crown Square. It was laid out in the fifteenth century under James the Third, over great vaults that had been built in the fourteen thirties. The royal buildings on its sides gave the castle its political centre.

On the north side is the Scottish National War Memorial. This is a place with a long history. It stands on the site of the medieval church of St Mary, and it was designed by Robert Lorimer, who was appointed in nineteen nineteen. Building began in nineteen twenty three, the same year the army garrison left the castle, and the Prince of Wales opened it on the fourteenth of July nineteen twenty seven.

Inside, the Shrine holds a sealed casket containing Rolls of Honour that list more than a hundred and forty seven thousand Scots who died in the First World War. About fifty thousand more names from the Second World War are in the Hall.

It is a quiet place. Go in, even if only for a few minutes.

13:50

10Crown Room and the royal birth room

What to look for

  • The vaulted Crown Room on the first floor of the palace.
  • The small Birth Chamber on the ground floor, with its painted ceiling of sixteen seventeen.

The Royal Palace is on the east side of Crown Square. Two things to see inside, and each one has a good story.

First, the Crown Room, home of the Honours of Scotland. The Honours are the Crown, the Sceptre and the Sword of State, and they're the oldest surviving crown jewels in the British Isles. The crown was made in Scotland and has looked as it does now since fifteen forty. The sword and the sceptre were made in Italy, as gifts to James the Fourth from the Pope. They were used together at Scottish coronations from Mary, Queen of Scots in fifteen forty three to Charles the Second in sixteen fifty one. And even after the Union of the Crowns, they sat on a table in the Parliament of Scotland to show the monarch was present.

After the Union of seventeen oh seven, the Honours were locked in a chest in this castle, and people eventually thought they had been lost or taken to London. In eighteen eighteen, Sir Walter Scott was given permission to search. He broke into a sealed room, which is the Crown Room, and unlocked the chest inside. The Honours were there. They went on public display at a charge of one shilling. Four years later, in eighteen twenty two, the Honours were used for the state visit of King George the Fourth, and they have been on show here ever since.

Second, the tiny Birth Chamber. It was here, on the nineteenth of June fifteen sixty six, that Mary, Queen of Scots gave birth to her son. He became James the Sixth of Scotland and, in sixteen oh three, James the First of England, joining the two crowns. The painted ceiling and decoration you see was added in sixteen seventeen, when James came back for a visit and the palace was refurbished for him. Even then he preferred to sleep at Holyrood.

One thing you will not see: the Stone of Destiny, also called the Stone of Scone. It sat here from nineteen ninety six, when it came back to Scotland after seven hundred years in England. Edward the First had taken it in twelve ninety six. It moved to Perth Museum in March twenty twenty four.

14:20

11The Great Hall

What to look for

  • Look up at the hammerbeam roof.
  • The carved stone corbels that carry the roof timbers.

The Great Hall is on the south side of Crown Square. King James the Fourth built it, and it was finished in the early sixteenth century, between about fifteen oh nine and fifteen eleven, for banquets and state occasions. It's twenty nine metres long and a bit over twelve wide.

Look up. The hammerbeam roof is original, and it's one of only two medieval halls in Scotland that still have one. The castle's own research found that the oak was felled in Norway around fifteen ten and shipped here.

Now look at the carved stone corbels that carry the roof. Their Renaissance detail has been compared with work at Blois in France from around fifteen fifteen. That tells you something about Scotland at this date. It wasn't a remote corner of Europe. It was a court in touch with the newest ideas in France, and James the Fourth held tournaments at this castle.

A quick myth to drop. People often say the Scottish Parliament met here. There's no evidence that it did.

The hall's later story is less glamorous. It was used as a barracks, with soldiers sleeping in a room built for kings. In the eighteen eighties, the hall was restored, again with money from William Nelson and the work of Hippolyte Blanc.

14:40

12Half Moon Battery and David's Tower

What to look for

  • The curved wall of the battery, and the palace block beside it.
  • Look east along the Royal Mile towards Holyrood.

Head out to the Half Moon Battery on the east side. The big curved wall you're standing on was built by Regent Morton between fifteen seventy three and fifteen eighty eight, after the Lang Siege. Historians think it was out of date as a defence even when it was new. Its real effect was to hide the old tower behind it, so that the royal palace beside it looked more important.

Because beneath it lie the remains of David's Tower. David the Second began it around thirteen sixty seven. He died in thirteen seventy one before it was finished, and Robert the Second completed it. It was the heart of the castle for the next two hundred years, and it has a dark history.

In November fourteen forty, the sixteen year old Earl of Douglas and his younger brother David were summoned to the castle. After a meal in this tower, known ever since as the Black Dinner, both boys were seized and executed on trumped up charges. It was a plot by the Chancellor, Sir William Crichton, to break the power of the Douglas family.

In fourteen seventy nine, the king's brother, the Duke of Albany, was held in this same tower for plotting against King James the Third. He got his guards drunk, and then lowered himself out of a window on a rope. It worked, and he escaped to France.

The Lang Siege destroyed the tower in fifteen seventy three, when its south wall collapsed under the cannon fire. The Half Moon Battery was built over the ruins. During the Second World War, the Honours of Scotland were hidden down in what was left of it.

Look east from the battery along the Royal Mile. At the far end is Holyrood. Around the end of the fifteenth century, James the Fourth built the Palace of Holyroodhouse down there, and from then on the royal family preferred to stay at the abbey. That's when the castle's role as a home began to fade, and it became a fortress and a barracks. In eighteen twenty two, George the Fourth stood on this very battery and waved to the crowds during his visit to Edinburgh.

14:55

13The Prisons of War

What to look for

  • The recreated vaults, the chained doors and the fireplace in the French prisoners' vault.

Your last stop is the Prisons of War, recreated vaults where prisoners captured in wars were held. It's dark inside, and you'll hear audio recordings of people's voices, so give it a little patience.

The vaults held prisoners from the Seven Years' War, from seventeen fifty six to seventeen sixty three, from the American War of Independence, and from the Napoleonic Wars. Look for the fireplace in the French prisoners' vault, and the chained prison doors.

There was a famous escape. In eighteen eleven, forty nine prisoners got out through a hole in the south wall. That persuaded the authorities that the vaults were no longer a safe prison. Their use ended in eighteen fourteen, and from then on the castle slowly began to change into the national monument you see today.

The castle kept a prison role in other ways. The Marquis of Argyll was held here in sixteen sixty one. In sixteen eighty one his son, the ninth Earl of Argyll, escaped disguised as his sister's footman, though he was recaptured. In the First World War the Red Clydesider David Kirkwood was held in the military prison block, and in the Second World War, downed German pilots were held here.

15:15

Leaving, and on to Calton Hill

That's the castle. If it's about a quarter past three, start walking out now.

One last piece of history as you go. The last time this castle fought a real battle was seventeen forty five. Bonnie Prince Charlie took Edinburgh in September, but the deputy governor, George Preston, refused to give up the castle. The Jacobites tried to blockade it, Preston shelled the town, and the blockade was called off. Charles marched into England by November. Since then, the castle has been a barracks, a museum and a monument. Today about two million people a year walk through that gate, and you were two of them.

Calton Hill is about twenty five minutes away on foot, down the Royal Mile and along Princes Street. Sunset is about four o'clock. When you get to the top, look for the tall tower, the Nelson Monument. It was built between eighteen oh seven and eighteen sixteen for Nelson's victory at Trafalgar, and the time ball on top of it still drops at one o'clock, in step with the gun you heard from the castle.

Enjoy the evening.
