The Royal Mile and the Heart of Midlothian (14:00)
You're standing on the Royal Mile, the main street of the Old Town. It runs between Edinburgh Castle and Holyrood Palace and it's almost exactly one mile long. The name is quite modern. It only goes back to the early twentieth century.
Look down for the heart shaped mosaic made of coloured granite setts, the Heart of Midlothian. It marks the entrance to the Old Tolbooth, which stood here for more than four hundred years. The Tolbooth was the town hall, the meeting place for parliament at times, and the main jail, where punishment and torture were routine. It was pulled down in eighteen seventeen. Sir Walter Scott then wrote a novel about it in eighteen eighteen, The Heart of Midlothian, and the mosaic has marked the spot since eighteen sixty.
Locals often spit on the heart for good luck. It may have begun as a way of showing contempt for the executions. And the Edinburgh football club Hearts took its name and badge from this mosaic.
Beside the cathedral is the Mercat Cross, the old market cross, where official proclamations are read. It's first mentioned in a charter of thirteen sixty five. The Grassmarket (15:25)
The Grassmarket sits in a valley at the south west foot of the castle rock. In thirteen sixty three it's mentioned as the street called Newbygging, new buildings, under the castle. From fourteen seventy seven it was one of the main market places of Edinburgh, and part of it was for the sale of horses and cattle. The name probably comes from livestock grazing in pens beyond its western end.
It also has a darker history. It was the traditional place of execution. A memorial near the gibbet site was made in nineteen thirty seven for more than a hundred Covenanters who died on the gallows between sixteen sixty one and sixteen eighty eight, in a time called the Killing Time. In seventeen thirty six it was the setting for the Porteous Riots, which ended with the lynching of a captain of the Town Guard.
And there's the story of Maggie Dickson, a fishwife from Musselburgh, convicted of concealing a pregnancy and hanged here in August seventeen twenty four. The popular story is that she survived the hanging, and her name is now on a pub.
From eighteen hundred, many Irish immigrants settled here, in cheap lodging houses. It was also here, at Tanners Close at the west end, that most of the Burke and Hare murders took place in eighteen twenty eight.

The Vennel viewpoint (15:45)
In the south west corner, take the Vennel, the narrow lane that climbs from the Grassmarket. On its east side you can still see some of the best surviving parts of the old town walls, the Flodden Wall and the Telfer Wall.
The Flodden Wall was built after the Scots were beaten at the Battle of Flodden in fifteen thirteen, when the town feared an English attack. It was about one point two metres thick and up to seven metres high. It began at the castle, ran across the west end of the Grassmarket, and then went uphill along the Vennel, and a watch tower survives at this south west corner. It then wrapped around Greyfriars Kirkyard.
The walls were never very good at defence. They were more a way to control trade, tax goods and deter smugglers. By the mid eighteenth century they had outlived their purpose and sections were pulled down.
