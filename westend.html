09:55

Before you set off

Good morning. Today's first walk follows a river, and the river is the story. It's called the Water of Leith. It starts in the Pentland Hills, runs about twenty miles, and goes through the middle of Edinburgh on its way to the port of Leith. The name Leith may come from an old Brittonic word meaning damp or moist, which suits the weather.

You're starting close to the hotel. In about three hours you'll go from a medieval milling village, along the river, to the village of Stockbridge, and then up to a quiet cobbled lane in the New Town. Tap Next at each stop, or tap Find my stop and the page picks the closest one.

Take your time. The castle is booked for twelve thirty, so the plan is to be climbing towards the Esplanade by about twelve.

10:00

1Dean Bridge

What to look for

  • Four tall arches carrying the road across the valley.
  • Down below on your right as you face north, the roofs of Dean Village.

You're standing on the Dean Bridge. Look over the parapet. The river is a long way down, about a hundred and six feet, or thirty two metres, below the road. That's the point of the bridge.

It was designed by Thomas Telford, one of the great civil engineers of the age, and finished in eighteen thirty one when he was seventy three. It was one of the last major works before he retired. It's four hundred and forty seven feet long, carried on four arches, each spanning ninety feet.

There's a nice detail. Telford first planned three arches. The ground on the south bank was poor, so he changed the design to four smaller ones.

Before the bridge, the river was crossed by a ford, and later by a single arch stone bridge at the foot of a steep street called Bell's Brae. Anyone travelling from Edinburgh to Queensferry had to go down into this valley and up the other side. Walk down to the village now, by the steps or by Bell's Brae.

10:10

2Dean Village and Well Court

What to look for

  • Well Court, the tall red sandstone housing block with the clock tower.
  • The old bridge over the river at the foot of Bell's Brae.

This is Dean Village. Dene is an old word for a deep valley, and you can feel it. The village was called the Water of Leith Village at one time, and it worked as a grain milling area for more than eight hundred years. At one time there were no fewer than eleven working mills here, all driven by the river.

The oldest record is from around eleven forty five. King David the First granted one of his mills of Dene to Holyrood Abbey in the abbey's founding charter. So a king gave away a mill in this exact valley nearly nine hundred years ago.

The village was tied to the bakers of Edinburgh. The Incorporation of Baxters, the bakers' guild, built a large granary here in sixteen seventy five, and part of it served as the village tolbooth.

The most striking building is Well Court. It was built as housing for workers in the eighteen eighties and designed by the architect Sydney Mitchell. Housing built for the workers, rather than for the owners, is worth noticing.

The bridge across the river at the foot of Bell's Brae is believed to stand on the site of very ancient crossings. The present bridge is early eighteenth century, built as a single arch wide enough for a carriage and horses, on the route from Edinburgh to Queensferry.

10:30

3The Water of Leith Path

What to look for

  • Follow the signs for the Water of Leith Walkway downstream, with the river on your left.
  • Fast water and old weirs. Those were the mill lades.

Now join the Water of Leith Walkway and follow it downstream, with the river on your left. The walkway is a long one, twelve point eight miles from Balerno to Leith, and it passes through woodland, often well away from roads. Today you only walk a short section of it, towards Stockbridge.

As you walk, listen to the river. Every weir and every drop in the level was once a source of power. This short stretch, between the West End and Dean Village, was a gorge of watermills. That's why a river of this size drove eleven mills in one village.

The ravine you're in is the reason Edinburgh's grandest road bridge, the one you stood on, had to be so high. Look back at it once you're a little way along.

Further on, the river passes Stockbridge, then Inverleith, Canonmills and Warriston, then widens into the old harbour at Leith. You aren't going that far today, but it's a nice thing to know. This little stream ends at the sea.

10:45

4St Bernard's Well

What to look for

  • A round, columned temple on the river bank, with a statue inside.
  • It's reached by a stair off St Bernard's Bridge.

Beside the river you'll see a small round temple with columns, St Bernard's Well. It covers a spring beside the Water of Leith. The painter and designer Alexander Nasmyth drew up the circular temple in seventeen eighty nine.

It's easy to walk past a building like this and take it for a folly. It's a classical temple built over a spring, from the age when Edinburgh was proud of being a modern, classical city.

The well sits on the old estate of Henry Raeburn, the portrait painter, who owned two adjoining estates here, Deanhaugh and St Bernard's. He developed them with the architect James Milne. That's the reason the streets above you have such an ordered, planned feel.

Check whether it's open when you get there. If it isn't, the outside is the thing to see.

10:55

5Stockbridge

What to look for

  • The Stock Bridge itself, a stone bridge over the water.
  • St Stephen's Church with its tall tower, on the way up to Circus Lane.

Welcome to Stockbridge. The name is Scots for stock brig, a timber bridge. It began as a small outlying village, and was taken into the city in the nineteenth century. The stone bridge you see now, the Stock Bridge, was built in eighteen oh one.

Stockbridge is still a village in feeling. The main street is Raeburn Place, a mix of small shops, named for Henry Raeburn. It's also where the first international rugby match, Scotland against England, was played, on the playing fields that share the street's name.

This is a good place for coffee. Take twenty minutes. When you're ready, walk uphill into the New Town, past St Stephen's Church. It was designed by William Playfair in eighteen twenty seven, and it's squeezed onto a site where the ground falls steeply. It was meant to stand in the centre of a circus, and ended up at the end of a street instead.

Just to the north of the church is the old Stockbridge Market, where the original entrance archway still stands.

11:05

6Circus Lane

What to look for

  • A curving cobbled lane of small houses, with the planted fronts and pots.

Your last stop is Circus Lane. It runs parallel to St Stephen Street, a mews lane where old and new buildings sit together. And that's the interesting thing. This was a service lane. It was built as a back street, so the coaches and horses of the big New Town houses could be kept out of sight.

That explains the look. The buildings are small, low and close together, because they were stables and coach houses, not family homes. Today old and new buildings sit side by side along it.

It's a quiet place, so be kind to the people who live here. Take your photos, then head on. From here it's about twenty five minutes on foot to Princes Street Gardens, and you'll want to be at Ross Fountain by about half past eleven.
