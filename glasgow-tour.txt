Glasgow city walk, Self-guided audio walk, Mon 16 Nov, about 12:00 to 15:30

Before you start (11:55)
Glasgow isn't Edinburgh, and that's the fun of it. Edinburgh is a medieval ridge and a Georgian New Town. Glasgow is a Victorian trading city that grew rich on the Clyde, first on tobacco, then on shipbuilding and heavy industry. The buildings are big, confident and a bit showy, and the people are famously friendly.
Today's route is a loop from your hotel. You'll start at the most famous traffic cone in Britain, cross George Square, stop for lunch in the Merchant City, then go up to the cathedral and the Necropolis in the best of the afternoon light.
The sun sets at about four, so the hill at the end is timed for the last good light. Wear shoes with grip. The Necropolis paths are steep.

Gallery of Modern Art and the Duke of Wellington (12:00)
You're at Royal Exchange Square, about ten minutes on foot from the hotel, and the Gallery of Modern Art. You don't need to go in, though it's free and open from ten on Mondays. What you came for is outside.
In front of the gallery stands an equestrian statue of the Duke of Wellington, made by the sculptor Carlo Marochetti and put up in eighteen forty four. For years now, the Duke has worn a traffic cone on his head. The council has tried to stop it, and the cone keeps coming back. It has been painted gold to celebrate big sporting wins, and it has become an unofficial symbol of the city's humour.
The building behind him began as a townhouse in the seventeen seventies, built for William Cunninghame, one of Glasgow's wealthy tobacco merchants. It later served as the Royal Exchange, and then as a library. It reopened as the gallery in nineteen ninety six.
Take your photo, and then carry on east to George Square.

George Square and the City Chambers (12:20)
This is George Square, the civic heart of Glasgow. The grand building on the east side is the City Chambers, designed by William Young and finished in eighteen eighty eight. It was built to show how rich and important Glasgow was, and it does the job. Inside there are marble staircases and a banqueting hall.
There are free guided tours on weekdays at ten thirty and two thirty, about forty five minutes, with tickets from the front desk. The two thirty tour clashes with your cathedral time, so today the outside is the show.
The square also holds the city's war memorial, the cenotaph, and statues of famous Scots. Queen Street station is just beside you, and it's the one you arrived at yesterday.

Lunch in the Merchant City (12:45)
Now it's lunchtime, in the Merchant City. This district was laid out in the seventeen hundreds for the merchants who made fortunes importing tobacco from Virginia and Maryland. The trade collapsed when the American War of Independence began, but the wealth had already built a lot of the city centre.
Today it's full of cafes, restaurants and bars, so pick whichever looks right. It's about a ten minute walk from George Square along Ingram Street, so you should be sitting down by about quarter to one.
After lunch, walk east to Glasgow Cross, where you'll see the old Tolbooth Steeple, a tall tower from the early sixteen hundreds that is all that remains of the town's old tolbooth. From there, follow the High Street uphill to the cathedral. Allow about twenty minutes, and aim to arrive by quarter to two.

Glasgow Cathedral (13:45)
This is Glasgow Cathedral, also called the High Kirk or St Mungo's. It's the oldest cathedral on the Scottish mainland and the oldest building in Glasgow, dating back to the twelfth century. It's also the most complete medieval cathedral on the mainland.
It stands where St Mungo, also known as Kentigern, is thought to have been buried in the year six hundred and twelve. He's Glasgow's patron saint. His tomb is in the lower church, which is the part to see if you only have time for one thing.
Entry is free, and donations go to Historic Environment Scotland. In winter it's open from ten to four, Monday to Saturday. Last entry is around three, and the lower church shuts at about quarter past three. It can close for services or for lunch, so don't leave it late. An audio guide costs two pounds if you want more detail.
Give it forty five minutes. Then head out the side, because the next stop is on the hill right beside it.

Glasgow Necropolis (14:30)
A footbridge by the cathedral takes you to the Necropolis, a Victorian garden cemetery on a steep hill. It's free to walk around.
It was planned by the Merchants' House of Glasgow from eighteen thirty one, and it opened in eighteen thirty three. It was modelled on Père Lachaise in Paris, and it was the first garden cemetery in Scotland. About fifty thousand people are buried here, in around three and a half thousand tombs.
At the top is a sixty foot column, topped by a statue of the reformer John Knox, which went up in eighteen twenty five, before the cemetery existed. Knox isn't buried here. The richest families paid for plots near the summit, while the humbler graves sit on the lower terraces. Death had a class system too.
Climb to the top, and then turn around. You'll be looking back over the cathedral roof and across the city. It's one of the best views in Glasgow, and the light should be golden by about three.
The paths are steep and can be slippery. I couldn't confirm the closing time, so be down by about half past three, before it gets dark.

Back to the hotel (15:30)
That's the loop done. The way back to the hotel is about thirty minutes on foot, downhill most of the way. Go down through the Merchant City, or via George Square and Buchanan Street if you want to window shop. A taxi is a good idea if your feet are tired.
You've got a break before the concert. The train from Central to the Exhibition Centre leaves around half past five. Enjoy the show.
