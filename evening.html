Before you start

You've had the sunset. Now come down from Calton Hill, and walk along Princes Street towards St Andrew Square. The first stop is a Victorian Gothic spire and the second is the Georgian heart of the New Town, and they're only a few minutes' walk from each other.

16:30

1Scott Monument

What to look for

  • The statue of Scott and his dog Maida under the central arch.
  • The small figures of characters from his novels, sixty four of them.

This is the Scott Monument, a Victorian Gothic monument to the author Sir Walter Scott. It's the second largest monument to a writer in the world, after the one to José Martí in Havana. Waverley Station, just beside it, is named after Scott's Waverley novels.

It's two hundred feet and six inches high, which is just over sixty one metres, and made of Binny sandstone from West Lothian. If you climb, the highest platform is two hundred and eighty seven steps up. I've seen conflicting opening hours, so I wouldn't count on the climb at this hour. The outside is the show.

Here's the story. After Scott died in eighteen thirty two, there was a competition for a design. An unlikely entrant used a made up name. He was George Meikle Kemp, a forty five year old joiner and self taught architect, who feared his lack of qualifications would rule him out. The judges picked his design in eighteen thirty eight.

The foundation stone was laid on the fifteenth of August eighteen forty. The building was finished in the autumn of eighteen forty four, at a cost of just over sixteen thousand pounds. But Kemp never saw it complete. He fell into the Union Canal while walking home from the site and drowned, before the monument was opened in eighteen forty six.

Under the central arch, John Steell's white Carrara marble statue shows Scott seated, with a quill pen, and his dog Maida by his side. Around the tower are sixty four figures of characters from his novels, carved by a team of Scottish sculptors.

16:50

2St Andrew Square

What to look for

  • The tall fluted column in the middle of the gardens.
  • Dundas House on the east side, now a bank building.

A few minutes' walk brings you to St Andrew Square, the east end of George Street. Building began in seventeen seventy two, as the first part of the New Town, designed by James Craig. Within six years of its completion it was one of the most desirable and fashionable residential areas in the city.

By the end of the nineteenth century it had turned into a commercial centre, home to banks and insurance companies, and at one time the square could claim to be the richest area of its size in Scotland. The gardens have been open to the public only since two thousand and eight.

In the middle stands the Melville Monument, a tall fluted column to Henry Dundas, the first Viscount Melville, who was a dominant figure in British politics in the late eighteenth century. It was built between eighteen twenty one and eighteen twenty seven, and was pushed through by Royal Navy officers. In twenty twenty, after protests, the council added a plaque saying that Dundas deferred the abolition of the Atlantic slave trade. It's one of the most debated monuments in Scotland, and the plaque is worth reading.

On the east side is Dundas House, built for Sir Lawrence Dundas between seventeen seventy two and seventeen seventy four by the architect Sir William Chambers. It became the head office of the Royal Bank of Scotland in eighteen twenty five, and its Palladian front appears on the bank's banknotes.

That's the end of the tour. George Street is right here for dinner, and Saturday night fills up quickly, so it's worth having a table booked.
